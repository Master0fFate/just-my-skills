# Uncluttered design

Everything designed is seen one frame at a time: a poster, a slide, a chart, one screen of an app, one section of a page. A frame gets a small amount of attention (a glance for a poster or chart, a few seconds for a slide or the top of a landing page, a quick scan for an app screen). In that time it can do one job: land one point, or get one action taken.

So: **each frame does one job, with everything the viewer needs for that job, and nothing else.**

Both halves count. "Nothing else" is where a first draft fails. "Everything the viewer needs" is where an over-corrected one fails.

Clutter is whatever in the frame is not working for its job. It is not the same as density. A price table or an admin list can be dense and uncluttered, because every cell gets used. A hero section with fifteen words can be cluttered, if eight of them are a tagline and a badge.

## Why your first draft will be cluttered

Asked only for "a tasteful graphic", models draw roughly 30 to 60 percent material an editor would cut (ClutterBench), and the same habits show up in pages, screens and slides. The clutter is not random. It comes from a few impulses that each feel like diligence while you are building. Learn to recognise them, because the feeling of "this adds value" is the symptom.

| The impulse | What it produces | What is true instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Coverage.** "This is also true and important." | A second chart, a stats row, a six-card feature grid, five bullets on a slide, summary tiles above every list. | The frame is not a summary of everything known. A second idea belongs in a second frame, or nowhere. |
| **Emphasis by repetition.** "Make sure they get it." | A result in the bars, a stat card and a takeaway line. A subhead that rewords the headline. The same button twice on one screen. "Settings" in the nav, the breadcrumb and the page title. | Said once, in its strongest form, a thing is clear. Said three times, the viewer has to work out that it is one thing. |
| **Template habits.** "This is what a designed thing has." | Eyebrow pills, category tags, badges, "trusted by" strips, numbered cards, a fat footer, slide footers with date and page number. | Those are parts of other people's layouts. A part belongs only if this frame's job needs it. |
| **Fear of empty space.** "That area looks unfinished." | Filler cards, decorative blobs and gradients, stock illustrations, an icon beside every heading. | Space left over means the main element is too small. Enlarge it, or leave the margin. Space is what makes the main element readable. |
| **Boxing.** "Containers look organised." | A card around every group, borders inside borders, tinted icon squares, dividers between everything. | Space and alignment group things. Every line and box is one more thing to look at. |
| **Copywriting voice.** "It should sound exciting." | Slogans ("Supercharge your workflow", "A warmup that moves the needle"), sentence-long headlines, exclamation marks. | Plain words that say what the thing is or what was found. The content is already the interesting part. |
| **Explaining.** "They might not follow." | A paragraph describing the chart, helper text under every field, "Manage your preferences here" under a Settings title. | If it needs explaining, fix the thing: a better label, a clearer form, fewer parts. |

None of this means the cut material is wrong or boring. It is cut because attention is limited, and every extra element spends some of it.

## Before you make anything: decide

Most clutter is decided before the first line of markup, by starting from "what do I know about this" instead of "what is this frame for". Settle these first, in your head or in notes. None of it is text for the design.

1. **The job, in one sentence.** For a frame that communicates: the point ("Heated docks lifted winter rides"). For a frame someone uses: the task they came to do ("see today's jobs and start the next one"). Take it from what the source or brief is about, not from whichever fact is easiest to make look impressive. If you need "and", you have two jobs.
2. **The primary element.** The one thing that carries the job: the chart, the headline with its button, the list of the user's own items, the form. It gets the area and the contrast. Everything else is support.
3. **What a stranger needs.** Imagine someone with no context. To read it: what is this about, what does each mark or label stand for, compared with what. To use it: where am I, what can I do here, what happens if I press this. Write that list. It is short.
4. **What you are leaving out.** Name the true, interesting things that are not going in this frame. Some move to another frame (the next slide, a lower section, a detail screen). Most go nowhere.

For something with many frames (a page, a deck, an app), decide the frames first. A frame exists because the viewer has a question or a task at that moment, in the order they would have it. A section is never added because pages usually have one.

## Sort every element: keep, shrink or cut

- **Keep** what does the job or orients the viewer.
- **Shrink** what belongs but takes more room or words than it should: the sentence that should be a label, the headline that should be half the size.
- **Cut** the rest. It comes in three kinds: *unneeded* (tells the viewer nothing they need), *a second idea* (serves a different job from this frame's), and *a repeat* (already said or shown elsewhere in the frame).

The rules that follow from this:

- **One primary of each kind per frame.** One headline, one focal visual, one primary action. A second action can exist, but it looks secondary (a text link, not another filled button).
- **Each thing once per frame.** One statement of each fact, figure, name and action. Repeating the main action at the end of a long page is fine; that is a new frame.
- **Labels, not sentences.** Headlines of about seven words. Buttons that name the action. Captions that are a phrase.
- **Orienting context stays, small and once.** What is measured, what product this is, which screen this is, when and where the event is. This is information, not clutter.
- **Group with space and alignment before lines and boxes.** A container is an element and has to earn its place like any other.
- **Decoration has to mean something.** Colour marks emphasis or state: one accent, used on the thing that matters. An icon stands in for a word, it does not sit beside the same word. No gradients, shadows, emoji or patterns for their own sake.
- **One brand mark.** Brand otherwise lives in typeface and colour, not in repeated names, taglines and URLs.

### Where the urge should go

| You want to add | Do this instead |
|---|---|
| A stat card, callout or takeaway line restating the result | The main element already says it. Put the figure in one place. |
| A subhead or paragraph explaining the main element | Make the labels do it. Then delete the sentence. |
| An eyebrow, kicker, category tag, pill or badge | Nothing. |
| A stats strip ("10k+ users · 99.9% uptime") | One figure in a sentence where it supports the point, if it is real and matters. |
| A grid of feature cards with icons | Show the one to three things that matter, each with a real picture of it. |
| Logos, testimonials, FAQ, because pages have them | Only real ones, and only if a visitor would be asking that question there. |
| A second filled button | Decide which action is the job. Make the other a link. |
| A welcome banner, or a subtitle under a screen title | The title is enough. Give the space to the content. |
| Helper text under a field | A clearer label. Keep help only where a mistake is likely or costly. |
| Method or "how it works" cards beside a result | Another frame, or the article's job. |
| A footer with wordmark, tagline and URL on a graphic or slide | You already have a logo. |
| An icon, illustration or shape to fill a gap | Enlarge the primary element, or leave the gap. |

## By surface

The same rule everywhere; what changes is the job and the usual offenders.

**Charts and data graphics.** The job is one finding. Keep the marks; a name and one value per mark; either a legend or direct labels; one short context line saying what is measured and on what ("Pass rate · Terminal-Bench 2.0"); a headline that names the finding like a title; a small on-chart annotation for a change, in place of a stat card; one small footnote if evaluation or source details matter to trust; one logo. Each figure appears once: if the bars are labelled "+41%", the headline does not say 41%. The chart takes about two thirds of the canvas. A clean single-chart graphic has 10 to 40 words. A headline claims no more than the data shows ("nearly matches" over bars of 34.6% and 41.3% claims too much).

**Diagrams.** The boxes and arrows are the marks. Name each box in one to three words. No paragraph under each box, no step numbers when arrows give the order, no icon beside a label that already says it. If one short fact per box is what makes the diagram worth reading (a deadline, an owner), give every box the same kind of fact. Seven boxes or fewer.

**Slides.** One claim per slide, stated in the title. The body is the single piece of evidence for it: one chart, one diagram, one number, or at most three short lines. The title makes the claim in words and the evidence carries the numbers, so a figure labelled on the chart is not repeated in the title. The rest is said aloud or goes on another slide. A row or column of other metrics beside the evidence is a second idea, even on a summary slide; if the audience needs those numbers, they get their own slide. No bullets that restate the title, no takeaway box, no footer furniture unless the brief requires it.

**Landing and marketing pages.** The first screen answers three questions: what is this, who is it for, what do I do next. That is a plain headline saying what the product does, one supporting line, one primary button, and one picture of the actual product. Below it, only sections that answer a visitor's next question, in the order they would ask (how does it work, why believe it, what does it cost, how do I start), each with one point and one visual. No eyebrow pill, no stats strip, no logo wall, no invented testimonials. A short nav and a footer with only what is needed.

**App screens and dashboards.** The job is the task the person opened the screen for, and the primary element is their own content: the list, the document, the form. One primary action, visually distinct. The title appears once. No welcome banner, no description under the title, no summary tiles unless a number there changes what the person does on this screen. An empty state is one line and the action. A dashboard leads with the answer to the question it is checked for. Density is fine when the person uses every row; clutter is what they skip every time.

**Posters, covers and social cards.** Three levels at most: what it is (large), the key facts such as when and where (clearly second, readable from a distance), how to act (small). A slogan can lead only if the name of the thing and its key facts are still unmissable. One image or one strong typographic idea, such as the date set huge. Everything else comes off: sponsor lists, statistics, history, second appeals.

## Do not strip it bare

Over-correction is a real failure, and it is quieter than clutter because the result looks clean. The test is the stranger: can they read it or use it with no other context? Typical ways a "minimal" design fails them:

- Two bars and two percentages, with no metric or subject named anywhere.
- A big "+5%" with no baseline: five percent more of what, than what?
- A hero with a mood line ("Work, reimagined") that never says what the product is.
- Icon-only buttons with no labels, or a form whose only labels are placeholders that vanish on typing.
- A poster with no date or place.
- A screen with no title, no sign of state, or no way back.
- An expert's table with half its columns removed because they looked busy.

The question for any element is not "could they survive without it" but "does it help or orient them". Small, plain, useful context stays.

## Uncluttered is not plain

Cutting leaves fewer elements, so each one has to be better. A poster that reads like a memo, or a page that looks like a wireframe, has swapped clutter for blandness, and that is also a failure.

Spend the effort you saved on the primary element. Make it large. Set the type with care: real contrast in size and weight between levels, tight headlines, comfortable body text. Use the brand's colour with confidence, in big areas, not as thin accents. Give the frame one distinctive idea built from its own content: an oversized number, a date used as the image, the product shown large, a strong shape made by the chart itself. Character comes from scale, type, colour and composition. It never needs extra parts.

## Layout: give the area to the primary element

Clutter is measured in area as much as in count, so an oversized headline is clutter even when its words are right.

- The primary element gets the most area and the strongest contrast. On a single-frame graphic that is about two thirds of the canvas.
- Hierarchy comes from size, weight and space, in two or three clear levels. If everything is emphasised, nothing is.
- When cutting leaves a gap, enlarge the primary element or keep the margin. Do not enlarge the text to fill it, and do not fill it.
- Quiet means small and plain, not faint. Small text still has to be readable: about 2% of a graphic's short side, and normal body sizes in an interface.
- Fill a stated canvas exactly. No scrolling in a fixed frame, nothing cut off.

## Then look at it, and delete

Clutter also creeps in while building, so the last step is an edit, done on the rendered result and not on the code.

**Render it at the target size and look at the image.** Use the preview tool the environment gives you. If there is none, a headless browser screenshot works:

```js
// node shot.cjs file.html 1200 1200
const { chromium } = require('playwright');
(async () => {
  const [file, w = 1200, h = 1200] = process.argv.slice(2);
  const b = await chromium.launch();
  const p = await b.newPage({ viewport: { width: +w, height: +h } });
  await p.goto('file://' + require('path').resolve(file));
  await p.screenshot({ path: 'preview.png' });
  await b.close();
})();
```

Then go through it in this order.

1. **Defects first.** They are not clutter, but they ruin a clean design. Is the logo actually visible (a white logo on a light background renders as nothing)? Does any text overlap, wrap badly or get cut off? Is any text too faint or too small? Do labels line up with what they name? Are things crowded in one place while space sits empty in another? Is there an empty box left from something you removed?
2. **Count.** Jobs per frame: one. Primary actions: one. Brand marks: one. Times each fact or figure appears: one. Levels of emphasis: two or three.
3. **Delete pass.** For each element, containers included: does it do the job or orient the viewer? Is it already said elsewhere? If it is neither needed nor unique, delete it.
4. **Stranger pass.** Look again as someone with no context. If you cannot tell what it is about, what a mark means or what a control does, add the few words that fix it.
5. Render again. Stop when a pass changes nothing.

## Worked examples

**A chart.** Source: a post where fine-tuning took a model's pass rate on Terminal-Bench 2.0 from 3.1% to 17.0%. First draft: a category tag, an eyebrow, the headline "A warmup that moves the needle.", a subtitle restating the numbers, the chart at a third of the canvas, two stat cards ("5.5x", "+10.4 points"), two method cards, a row of dataset counts, a footer with wordmark and URL. About 110 words, all true. After: the logo; "Over 5x on Terminal-Bench 2.0"; a context line with the metric and model; two large bars, 3.1% in grey and 17.0% in the accent colour; a footnote on the evaluation. About 25 words.

**A landing page's first screen.** Brief: an invoicing tool for freelancers. First draft: a pill ("New · Now in beta"), the headline "Invoicing, reimagined for the modern freelancer", a subhead rewording it, two filled buttons, a strip of three statistics, a row of feature icons, and a decorative gradient. After: the headline "Send an invoice in a minute", one line ("Fill in who and how much; we chase the payment"), one button ("Create an invoice"), and a picture of a real invoice in the product. How it works and the price are the next two sections.

**An app screen.** Brief: the home screen of a job app for a plumber's field team. First draft: a greeting banner, four summary tiles (jobs, hours, rating, earnings), a weather card, a tip of the day, then today's jobs, each in a card with an icon, a status badge and two buttons. After: the title "Today", then the three jobs in time order, each a row with the time, address, job type and one "Start" button on the next job; below, a small line "2 more tomorrow". Earnings and ratings live on their own screens.

## When the brief asks for more

If the person asks for something this skill would cut (a multi-panel infographic, a testimonial section, a footer with the URL, a dense dashboard), do what they asked. Their brief decides the content. Apply the same discipline inside it: one job per frame, each thing said once, context kept short, the primary element given the area.

And the content is not yours to improve. Every figure comes from the source. Do not invent statistics, testimonials, customer logos or features to fill a layout; if there is no proof to show, there is no proof section. Do not word a headline more strongly than the facts.
